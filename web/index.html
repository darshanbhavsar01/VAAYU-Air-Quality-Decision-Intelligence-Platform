The Problem

An MP cannot act on what they cannot see

A sitting Member of Parliament receives constant complaints about air quality, but has no ward-level, forward-looking evidence to act on. Existing dashboards show yesterday's city-average AQI — useful for headlines, useless for targeted intervention.

  • Which neighbourhood will be unhealthy tomorrow? Existing tools answer at city scale, not street scale.
  • Why is a hotspot forming? Stagnant air, traffic, or accumulation — there is no attribution to guide action.
  • What should be done, and where? Interventions stay city-wide, slow, and hard to hold accountable.
  • Can it even run? A district office needs near-zero budget, existing infrastructure, and no new hardware.
Our Solution

A decision layer on top of existing station data

VAAYU interpolates a fine-grained pollution surface from the national CPCB network, forecasts where it will breach the safe limit within 24 hours, explains the likely driver, and delivers it as a map, a citizen advisory, and a weekly report — for any city, added with a single line of configuration.

Neighbourhood hotspot maps

A roughly 1 km grid interpolated from monitoring stations, clipped to each city. Every cell carries a distance-to-nearest-station confidence indicator — an approximation is never presented as a measurement.

24-hour spike forecast

For each station, the predicted next-day maximum PM2.5 and the probability of breaching India's 60 µg/m³ safe limit — the advance warning an office can act on.

Likely-cause attribution

A transparent weather-driven heuristic maps conditions to human-readable drivers (stagnation, traffic, accumulation), labelled as likely drivers rather than measured source apportionment.

Local-language advisories

Plain-language citizen health guidance in English, Hindi, and the city's regional language — Kannada, Marathi, Tamil, Bengali, or Telugu.

Weekly MP report

An auto-generated per-constituency briefing: outlook, top hotspots, and concrete, locally-actionable intervention suggestions.

Ask VAAYU

A natural-language question box answered strictly from the live forecast data — "which city is worst tomorrow?", "compare Delhi and Mumbai".

The Models

How the forecast is made

One global set of models is trained across all cities, using city and station identifiers plus weather and temporal features, so a newly onboarded city benefits from patterns learned elsewhere. Evaluation uses a temporal split with the most recent period fully held out.

Spike classifier
A gradient-boosted tree that predicts the probability that a station's PM2.5 will breach the 60 µg/m³ safe limit within the next 24 hours, using lagged and rolling pollutant history, weather (current and forecast), and time-of-day and seasonal features.
PM2.5 regressor
A gradient-boosted tree that predicts the next-24-hour maximum PM2.5 value at each station — the number that drives the map's forecast layer and the hotspot ranking.
Spatial interpolation
Inverse-distance weighting converts per-station forecasts into a continuous grid surface for each city, with a per-cell confidence tier based on distance to the nearest monitoring station.
Language models
Google Gemini generates the citizen advisories and weekly MP reports, grounded strictly in each city's forecast data — the models answer only from the numbers provided, never from free recall.
